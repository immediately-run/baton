<Lede>Baton lets teams of people and AI agents build software together, each ticket moving
along a course the team defines in its repository. The same Baton suits a small team that wants
the simplest course that works, and a factory that needs strict security and quality-assurance
guarantees.</Lede>

**Baton coordinates the work, and holds the record no push may change.** It decides who may take
each ticket and keeps that claim alive with a lease. It moves each ticket along its course, and
shows the whole team live. It also owns the state that someone pushing to the repository must not
be able to write: each ticket's course history, the attestations that count as evidence, the
repository's policy, the grants and the owners' bars. Git holds the work itself: every ticket,
its body and its evidence. Baton does not do, verify or confine the work. Its permissions cover
its own state, its gates protect the lock, and it advises on everything else. A repository can opt
into one exception through its policy: Baton's trusted runners decide what lands, and the forge
lets only them and a human land it (BATON_SPEC §12.8).

## The problem

A team that coordinates people and agents through git alone pays costs that grow with the team
and with what is at stake:

- **A dead claim blocks a ticket for days.** Nothing watches liveness all the time, so a dead
  holder is found only when someone sweeps for one.
- **Eligibility is self-judged.** An agent decides for itself whether it can do a ticket, and
  only finds out it could not after claiming it.
- **People and agents work in different places.** A question for a person, a review, an approval
  and an agent's leg all travel through separate channels, and no single place shows what is
  waiting on whom.
- **One process fits nobody.** A tool with a fixed process is too heavy for two people and a few
  agents, and too loose for a team that must prove who reviewed and approved what.
- **Anything in git can be rewritten by a push.** Once agents can push, a record kept only in git
  can be edited by the very agent it is meant to constrain: who approved a change, which review
  counted, what the rules are. A compromised runner included.
- **Nobody can see the factory.** Which workers are up, what each could take, what is running and
  what needs a person is pieced together after the fact, or not at all.

## What Baton gives a team

- **One fenced lease per ticket.** A worker claims a ticket and keeps it by heartbeat. A worker that goes silent loses the claim when its lease runs out, and a fence stops
  a revived one from writing.
- **People and agents on one course.** Agents run legs. A person takes part in two ways: by
  working a leg at a runner that declares `human-operator`, or by answering a person leg, the one
  way a course waits on a person (R-BTN-116). A suspended or released claim leaves a
  hand-off for whoever comes next. *Needs you* lists everything waiting on a person.
- **Courses, defined by the team.** A course is the team's workflow: the stations a ticket rests
  at and the legs it runs between them. Each repository keeps its courses in its `.baton/` folder
  and changes them with a commit, not a Baton deploy. Baton ships standard courses that cover the
  simple end; a team writes its own for the rest.
- **State no push can write.** Course history, attestations, the policy, grants and owner bars
  live in Baton. Some of it is copied into git for people to read, and some of it is in git
  nowhere. If Baton loses that state, it fails closed: the work is reviewed and approved again,
  never restored from git (the owner, 2026-10-28).
- **Gates protect the lock; the rest is advice.** Baton refuses only what would make cooperating
  workers collide, or take work they have said they cannot do: a second holder, a missing
  capability or seat, a tier above the worker's ceiling, a barred tag. Everything else it offers
  in a default order that a worker may override, and every override is logged.
- **A live view.** Workers, claims, timelines, what is ready, what is blocked and what needs a
  person, all on one dashboard, along with the course's own record of how long things take. Advice
  is never older than the last write Baton accepted for the runner reading it. Fleet-wide views
  catch up within one alarm cycle.

## From one machine to a factory

Baton scales from one person running a few agents on their own machine to a software factory with
a threat model of its own. Both ends run the same core. What differs is the repository's
**policy**: one versioned document, held in Baton and never read from git, that decides who lands
on which refs, who approves what, and whose attestations count (BATON_SPEC R-BTN-188).

- **The simple end.** The `cooperative` preset and the standard courses: leases, courses and the
  live view, for workers that cooperate. At this end, owner bars and positions are a
  coordination record, not a boundary, and no guarantee is required.
- **The strict end.** The `protected` and `protected-hardware` presets turn the same state into a
  boundary. Trusted runners land only what the policy's attestations allow. Reviews count only
  from independent domains, and human gates are satisfied only by a human-only key. A deployment
  that depends on Baton requires named **guarantees**, such as `no-runner-lands`,
  `independent-review`, `tickets-approved` and `human-gates`, and lists them as **floors**. Baton
  then refuses any policy change that would break one of them, and stops the repository while one
  is broken (R-BTN-189).
- **Everything between.** A preset is a starting point, not a mode: an owner applies one, then
  tunes it knob by knob. No rule reads a preset's name.

The courses Baton ships assume no particular architecture, and they serve the simpler end. A
factory whose guarantees rest on its own architecture writes the courses that rely on it and keeps
them itself: immediately.run's courses are Fieldhouse's (the owner, 2026-10-29).

## Who it is for

Teams of people, agents or both that work on the same repositories, from a solo developer with a
handful of agents to a factory of several dozen workers. It also serves the operator, who needs
to see at a glance what the team is doing and what it needs from a person.

## What it is not

- **Not the record of the work.** Git holds every ticket and its evidence, and Baton's board is a
  cache of it, rebuilt by each sync. Baton holds the live leases, the course history,
  attestations and the limits agents cannot change. When Baton and git disagree about what Baton
  owns, Baton wins; about anything else, git wins.
- **Not a gatekeeper of the work.** Baton never verifies or confines what a runner does. A
  capability, `human-operator` included, is the runner's own statement, which Baton records and
  matches but never grants or checks. A threat that needs a runner stopped belongs to the
  sandbox, the forge or the credential proxy. The one exception is the trusted-runner merge a
  policy opts into. A repository chooses to adopt Baton; once it has, Baton's refusals bind every
  worker that uses it, and a worker that ignores them is outside the factory, not stopped by it
  (the owner, 2026-10-22).
- **Not a workflow engine.** A course says where a ticket may go next; workers decide how to run
  each leg. Baton runs no repository code. For durable execution of your own code, use something
  like Temporal ([compared](../compared-to-temporal.mdx)).
- **Not tied to GitHub.** Any git forge, or a plain git server, will do.
